## 1. Early Writing: Mark-making, Notation Systems, and Scripts 3000-500 BCE The design of proto-writing systems introduced the first stable codes for graphic representation of language. The distinction of mark and interval on a deliberately identified surface is the basis of any graphic communication system. The elements of visual sign systems were defined by fundamental graphic variables: shape, size, orientation, and position. The meaningful organization of signs depended on compositional principles of juxtaposition, sequence, hierarchy, and direction. Pictorial signs were first used to represent concrete things but quickly came to function as a means of encoding ideas as well. Literate culture developed from the capacity to represent language in a visual code agreed upon as a convention by a social group. The effects of writing as a form of social control and power spring from this consensus. 1.1 Marks as signatures, North America, seventeenth century. Signatures in the shape of totem animals attest to the authority of this document. The picture signs are not writing in the proper sense. They stand directly for a person not for language. These marks are "like" writing, but they are not part of a code that can be used to represent or express other statements. Graphic media entered a new age with the coming of writing. By 3000 BCE, new tools for carving, inscribing, and marking were being developed and used on material supports of varying durability, such as clay, stone, papyrus, skin, bone, wax, metal, and wood. But the invention of writing required more than tools. Writing depended on the design of a stable visual code for the representation of language. This connection to language makes writing distinct from other visual signs that stand for ideas or things but not for speech or a linguistic system. Writing extended the principles of graphic organization that had been developed for image and symbol making. The shapes of the first glyphs, letters, and signs connect the earliest human marks to present-day alphabetic and character-based scripts through a continuous legacy. We still use versions of some of the earliest written symbols ever invented. From the outset, these symbols had the effect of changing the power of language by aligning it with the administration of culture. Writing orchestrated economic, political, religious, and other social activities. It expressed laws and social constraints that could be enforced by symbolic power rather than brute force. Whereas language distinguishes humans from other animals, writing introduces significant differences between oral and literate cultures. Oral communication is ephemeral and takes place in real-time circumstances that rely on presence. Writing communicates in the absence of its author. Enduring inscriptions transmit textual records to remote contexts (Fig. 1.1). Mark-making Mark-making is the most basic form of graphic expression and design. Drawing a line in the sand or making a handprint on a wall is a direct sign of individual identity. Such imprints have a curious existence: a mark that is a sign of the self is also always other than the self. 1.2 Rongo Rongo script, Easter Island, date unknown. The Rongo Rongo script of Easter Island has an intriguingiy exuberant rhythmic quality, but it has never been deciphered. Anthropologists now consider that it is probably not a written language but a set of signs used for ritual purposes. The script is not ancient, and scholars have suggested that it was invented by indigenous people exposed to European writing in the eighteenth century. Independent inventions of writing have occurred in many cultures. The graphic properties of this notation make it scriptlike because it contains marks and intervals that are clearly differentiated and nonpictorial elements that are neither physical traces of a hand or tool nor repeated motifs in a decorative pattern. ![](images/eab3e1f56971a4e87e648d2e3350269a03f778fbf135cc0a4422a04aac820922.jpg) ![](images/8f3eca94164d9e7077d49ec97bf63b32cba4c45c826eef3ab5fb705ab098ad57.jpg) Once made, marks exist independently of their makers. A line makes a division, whether in physical space as a boundary or in an intellectual zone to define a category. Differentiating between marked and unmarked things is a conceptual act of enormous significance. We might even suggest that the idea of difference forms the basis of human knowledge, and that the making of a mark is the primary way of inscribing such difference. On this simple principle of differentiation we can structure the complicated oppositions of I/Thou, me/you, this/that, self/other, subject/object, which form our understanding of the world. This relation 1.3 Painted stones, Mas-d'Azil, France, 10,000 sce. These are some of the earliest remains of systematic markmaking. The regularity of the repeated marks and their careful graphic execution indicate deliberate intention. Though too few in number to represent a language, they may have been conventionalized representations of things. The stick-figure form is referred to as the "hailelujah man" because it appears to be a person with arms raised in jubilation. This sign, like the other simple forms on these rocks, is common to many so-called primitive systems of marks. Even when they develop independently, human scripts often share formal characteristics. This does not suggest that a universal code underlies all writing but rather, pragmatically, that simple lines can be combined in a certain number of fairly predictable ways. Perhaps even more profoundly, in any graphic code, marks are different from the intervals that separate them (Fig. 1.2). Marks and signs alone do not constitute writing. The earliest marked objects that remain from prehistoric periods are the stones from the Mas-d'Azil area in France. They present enough regularity in their repeated use of crosses, waves, and sinewy patterns to suggest a conventional code. But the meaning of these marks relied on a context now lost, and the system to which they belonged seems insufficiently developed to represent a language. These stones may have been signs for objects rather than signs for language. Their designs do not appear to be numerous or varied enough to be a verbal code. This contrast is crucial. True writing is a graphic expression of language. This relation makes writing unique among visual forms of expression and fundamentally different from drawing or painting. But systems on the verge of becoming writing reveal the basic conditions for all letter and notation design: a deliberately identified surface, distinctions between figure and ground, differentiation of mark and interval, and principles of visual organization (sequence, scale, orientation, and juxtaposition) (Fig. 1.3). 1.4 Clay tokens, Mesopotamia, fourth millennium Bce. These regularly shaped objects served as a form of notation for business transactions. Their shapes each corresponded to a different category of agricultural product. The marks on their surfaces were highly schematic and used for detailed accounting. When these tokens were pressed into wet clay, their imprints were part of a graphic system in which relative size, shape, orientation, and groupings were all significant. These tokens demonstrate the emergence of a set of sophisticated graphic principles. The spatial organization of the surface and the elaboration of rules governing the use of visual signs are evidence of graphic refinement. This notation system is still not considered writing because "writing" is reserved for the representation of language, and these tokens are basically cash register receipts. The impressions made by the tokens were precursors to the wedge-shaped signs of cuneiform, the oldest system of actual writing (Fig. 1.4). During the several thousand years in which they were used, the signs on these clay tokens made a critical conceptual leap. At first, they were meant to represent specific things in schematic images. Then they came to function as abstract representations of categories of things or ideas. For instance, from the practice of continually representing four oxen, four sacks of grain, or four containers of oil came the idea that four could be represented by a sign. Thus, the concept of quantity was abstracted from direct, concrete counting. To the contemporary mind, the enormity of such a leap is almost impossible to appreciate. Yet to realize that many categories of thought are similarly abstract provides an insight into the contribution of notation and writing to human culture. The design problem implied in this shift is equally profound. What should a sign look like when it represents an abstract idea ("four-ness") rather than a thing? The transformation of Chinese characters from their pictorial origins to schematic signs happened with relative rapidity. Chinese characters most frequently represent either words or sounds, or some combination of the two. No writing system that required a sign for every word would be practical. The adaptation of characters for Japanese, Korean, and other languages demonstrates the versatility of Chinese script. The word notation refers to signs that stand for categories and ideas in a stable graphic system but fall short of the ability to represent language or speech. The term writing, as we have said, is reserved for graphic forms that specifically express language. The relation of writing to spoken language is not simple-even in a system such as the alphabet in which letter signs are supposed to represent sounds. Many things said in speech cannot be represented in writing, and vice versa. Written and spoken forms are both expressions of language. But they are not necessarily identical to each other. What is the written equivalent of an ironic inflection? A whisper? A seductive tone of voice? Writing has many properties that derive from its graphic nature. What is the spoken equivalent of a hyphen? Or a decorative initial? Or a sans serif face? Or the division of text into two columns on a page? cuneiform. The need for efficiency in writing systems propelled simplification from pictorial to schematic signs. A stylus in wet clay made some marks more easily than others, and the orientation as well as the shape and combination of these marks changed as writing became stabilized. Sumerian linear and cuneiform marks from about 2400 BcE were adopted by successive peoples in the same geographic regions: Akkadians, Babylonians, and Assyrians each used cuneiform for their own distinct language. Not only was cuneiform graphically efficient, but also it represented an enormous conceptual advancement: it was a fully developed, adaptable written code for language. indicate pictographic origins, but some debate exists about whether these signs derived from the more schematic imagery of token notation. In any case, these Sumerian pictograms themselves soon became schematized so that they could be drawn in a few quick strokes of the stylus on a soft tablet. The cuneiform script was adopted by the Akkadians, a Semiticspeaking people who invaded the Mesopotamian region about 2400 BCE. The adoption of a writing system developed for one language by another is a pattern that is repeated many times in the history of scripts. The Akkadians distilled a set of cuneiform signs that represented syllables. These voweland-consonant combinations were numerous but functioned more efficiently than signs for every word (Fig. 1.8). In a subsequent wave of migration into the same region around 1600 BCE, Hittites also adopted cuneiform, this time to represent their Indo-European language. The cuneiform script spread throughout the ancient Near East and was used extensively across various cultures and languages for diplomatic purposes in the third and second millennia BCE. The relationship between the visual aspects of a script and its origin is not always obvious. Writing consists of a set of signs made from easily replicated combinations of short straight and curved lines. Because their basic graphic ingredients are so limited, scripts can look similar but arise from very different historical contexts or geographical locations. Children and isolated cultures often make pictograms or other schematic signs. The shapes of these invented scripts can be remarkably like those of existing writing systems. Tracing the spread and transformation of writing requires attention to the functional as well as the formal design of scripts as systems Graphic form establishes stability in the representation and operation of language. The order and number of signs in a script are also part of its design as a system. But the tools and materials that produce and carry a writing system must be taken into account as well because they impose fundamental constraints on its graphic form. The task of devising a set of repeatable elements to be carved in stone is very different from that of impressing consistent signs in wet clay or forming them with a brush or pen. Although technology does not determine forms of writing, it may modify their design (Figs. 1.16a and 1.16b). 1.17 Hammurabi's Code, Iraq, eighteenth century Bce. In literate cultures, a record has its own existence, independent of its original context. The authority of the written word derives, in part, from this ability to circulate independently. The permanence of a written record lends its autonomy a power that is almost mythic. Writing can erase the marks of its origin. A code of law or a sacred text seems to transcend human authorship (Fig. 1.17). Myths and misconceptions continue to surround the history of writing. Many scholars place the beginnings of literacy at the time of the Greek alphabet's consolidation of vowel notation. Yet the list of cultural accomplishments and written texts that predate the advent of the Greek alphabet is a long one. On this list we would find the ancient Near Eastern tale of Gilgamesh with its legend of the Flood; the Babylonian Code of Hammurabi; the developed philosophical systems of India and Asia; successes of astronomy, navigation, cartography; civic structures and administration; and advanced arts of poetry. Suggestions that the Greeks were the first literate (and thus, first civilized) culture are based on misperceptions that are themselves historically determined, rather than grounded in archaeological fact. The distinction between oral and literate cultures is more significant than differences among versions of literacy. The shift from spoken word to written text is considered a prerequisite for other advances in technology, resource management, and social administration (Fig. 1.18). Conclusion The cultural impact of writing is so far-reaching that it was considered by many societies to have been a divine gift. Its invention seemed beyond the skills of any mere mortal. The creation of writing was attributed to the tracks of birds, to the constellations in the heavens, to the Indian god Ganesh, to the Egyptian deity Thoth, and to the tablets of Moses brought down from Mount Sinai. The Greeks attributed responsibility to Prometheus, who, according to legend, stole writing from the gods along with fire, thereby incurring a sentence of eternal torment. Clearly, writing marks and letters exert a tremendous force on human imagination. They also serve as an unlimited inspiration for exercises in graphic invention. Yet what is perhaps most remarkable about the letters of the alphabet is the degree of graphic stability that they have maintained for the several millennia since their invention. ## Early Writing:
